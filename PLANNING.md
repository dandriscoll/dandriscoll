# Plan Structure

## Why

Every plan begins with a clear purpose: the problem being solved or the outcome being achieved. Purpose is what every other part of the plan is measured against.

## Milestones

Break work into milestones that represent major steps toward the purpose. Each milestone should move meaningfully closer to the goal, and the path through them should be legible at a glance.

## Attention Is Finite

Planning exists because attention is finite. At any moment only a little work can hold real focus, so a plan's job is to decide where that focus goes: what deserves attention now, what should stay visible but less detailed, and what should be revisited later.

A plan is not just a record of tasks. It should actively point attention at the work that matters most.

## Detail Gradient

The detail gradient is an attention gradient. Specify each part of the plan in proportion to how close it is to action.

**Near-term**: Concrete and directive — closest to action, so it carries the most specificity. Clear enough to execute without further clarification.

**Mid-term**: Defines direction and scope. Detailed enough to guide upcoming choices, but not pinned down.

**Far-term**: High-level only — establishes what is in vs. out at each phase. Over-specifying distant work wastes attention and manufactures false certainty about decisions that aren't ripe yet.

## Choosing the Scope of Attention

Each planning session should first align on the right altitude for that moment. Sometimes the move is to narrow down onto the immediate next actions; sometimes it is to step back and revisit broader goals, milestones, or a project that has been neglected.

Don't simply follow whatever is most recently visible. Keep attention pointed at the most important work, not the most recent.

## Convergence

A good planning pass leaves the work in better shape than it found it. After a pass, the work should be clearer, more aligned with its purpose, more executable, less ambiguous, and less likely to drift.

Put plainly: each planning cycle should make the next useful action easier to identify than it was before. If a pass doesn't do that, it hasn't earned its keep.

## Role of the Planner

The planner helps keep attention on the work that matters. That includes gently redirecting it — when focus is stuck on low-value detail, drifting from the goal, or neglecting higher-priority work. This is part of good planning, not separate coaching.

Ask for signals about progress, feasibility, or priority only when they are genuinely needed for a quality plan. If a missing detail isn't essential, proceed with the best available understanding rather than stalling on questions. When the user gives direction, treat it as authoritative.
